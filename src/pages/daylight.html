Daylight

The Earth leans 23.4 degrees from upright, and over a year that lean turns first one hemisphere toward the Sun and then the other. The long days and short ones come from this alone.

How the figure is reached

For any day the page finds the Sun’s declination: how far north or south of the celestial equator it stands. It uses the Astronomical Almanac’s low-precision formulae, which are good to about a hundredth of a degree for the years around now.1 The sunrise equation then gives the angle the Earth must turn between sunrise and sunset:2

cos H₀ = (sin h₀ − sin φ sin δ) / (cos φ cos δ)

where φ is the latitude, δ the declination, and h₀ is −0.833°. Each fifteen degrees of turning is an hour, so the day lasts 2H₀ ÷ 15 hours. When the right-hand side falls below −1 the Sun never sets, and above +1 it never rises.

The −0.833° is why the equinox is not a day of twelve hours anywhere. Sunrise counts from the moment the Sun’s upper edge clears the horizon, not its centre, and the air bends its light over the horizon before it has truly arrived. Together these add some minutes to every day, and more the further you go from the equator.

The figure is the time between sunrise and sunset over a flat sea horizon. Hills, height above the sea and the weather will move a real sunrise by minutes.
