Chapter vii

The Islamic Year

Twelve Moons, and no correction for the Sun. The Islamic year lets its months run freely through the seasons, so that every feast in it visits winter and summer in turn.

The Islamic, or Hijri, calendar has twelve lunar months and a year of 354 or 355 days.1 Its era begins with the new year of AD 622, the year of Muhammad’s migration from Mecca to Medina, the Hijra.1 Unlike the Hebrew calendar, it adds no thirteenth month to keep step with the Sun: intercalation was forbidden in the tenth year of the Hijra, a prohibition Muslims take from the Qur’an, 9:36–37.1 So each date comes about eleven days earlier by the solar calendar than it did the year before.

Traditionally a month begins when the new crescent, the hilal, is first seen after sunset; if it is not seen on the evening of the 29th, the month runs to 30 days.1 Saudi Arabia’s Umm al-Qura calendar instead begins a month when, on the evening of the 29th, the conjunction has come before sunset and the Moon sets after the Sun at Mecca.1 This page uses a third form, the tabular calendar, which needs neither sky nor site: a fixed rule of month lengths in a thirty-year cycle. Historians use it to convert dates, and it can be a day or two early or late against the observed months.2

Twelve Moons

The months are 30 and 29 days long by turns, beginning with Muharram at 30. In leap years the last month, Dhu al-Hijja, has a 30th day.2 Twelve such months make 354 days, and twelve mean lunations . Thirty years

The tabular calendar adds its leap day in years 2, 5, 7, 10, 13, 16, 18, 21, 24, 26 and 29 of each thirty, eleven in all, so that thirty years hold 10,631 days.2 A short test gives the same years: year Y is leap when (14 + 11Y) mod 30 is less than 11. The thirty years hold 360 months, so the tabular month is 10,631 ÷ 360 = days, against for the mean synodic month.3 It is short by a month, and falls a whole day behind the mean Moon only once in about years.

The epoch is the civil one: 1 Muharram AH 1 was Friday 16 July 622 by the Julian calendar, 19 July by the Gregorian reckoned backwards.4 Some astronomical tables count from the day before. The Islamic day begins at sunset, so the date shown is the one that is current in daylight.

Through the seasons

The tabular year is days long on average, days short of the Gregorian year. A month therefore moves backwards through the solar calendar, coming round to the same season after Gregorian years, which are Islamic ones: the “about 33 years” of the usual account.1 The chart follows the first day of Ramadan, the month of fasting, from 1950 to 2100. Each dot is one year; each diagonal is one passage from December back to January.

The fast of Ramadan is kept from dawn to sunset, so the season it falls in sets its length. Near the equator that matters little. Far from it, a summer Ramadan has days hours longer than a winter one, and in seventy years the fast comes round through every season twice. Two clocks, one race

Because its year is shorter, the Islamic count gains on the Christian one, about of a year every year. Every thirty-three years or so a Gregorian year holds two Islamic new years, one in early January and the next in late December.
