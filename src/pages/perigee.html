Chapter xii

The Perigee

The Moon’s orbit is not a circle. Once a month it swings in to its perigee and out to its apogee, fifty thousand kilometres farther off. When the full moon happens to fall at the near point, the papers call it a supermoon. That meeting has its own cycle, a little under fourteen months.

Measured from the Earth’s centre, the Moon comes in as close as about 356,400 km and goes out as far as about 406,700.1 The time from one perigee to the next, the anomalistic month, is 27.5546 days, a few hours longer than the 27.3217 days the Moon takes to go once round against the stars, because the oval itself is turning. Its long axis, the line of apsides, creeps forward in the direction the Moon travels. The Moon gains one whole orbit on it after 1 ÷ (1/27.3217 − 1/27.5546) = … days, or … years.1

The full moon cycle

A full moon comes every 29.5306 days and a perigee every 27.5546, so after each full moon the perigee is about two days further ahead of it. The two meet again when the perigees have gained one whole lap: after 1 ÷ (1/27.5546 − 1/29.5306) = … days. That is fourteen full moons almost exactly, and fifteen perigees.3 Looked at another way, it is the time the Sun takes to come back to the turning line of apsides: the year against the stars, 365.2564 days, chasing a line that runs ahead once in … days, gives 1 ÷ (1/365.2564 − 1/…) = … days, the same number, because both are the same pair of motions counted from different ends.

“Supermoon” is not an astronomer’s word. The astrologer Richard Nolle coined it in 1979 for a new or full moon within 90% of the way to its nearest approach, and there is no agreed definition; by most, three or four full or new moons a year qualify.3 The plainer question has an exact answer: which full moon in each cycle comes nearest. Near and far are not fixed

Not every perigee is equally near. The chart below sets every perigee and apogee from 2000 to 2050 against the Moon’s phase at the time. Perigees at new and full moon are the deepest and those at the quarters the shallowest; apogees at new and full moon are the farthest, though over a much narrower range. So a full moon at perigee is doubly near: it catches the perigee, and the perigee is at its nearest then.

How much bigger

The two discs below are drawn to one scale: the nearest full moon and the farthest in the next fifteen years. The difference is real but modest. The width is about a seventh greater, the area and the light it sends about a third greater,3 and with nothing beside it in the sky to compare, few people could tell the two apart. A Moon low over the horizon looks far larger than either, but that is the Moon illusion, a trick of the eye, not of the orbit.3

The tides feel it more than the eye does. A nearer Moon pulls a little harder on the oceans, so a new or full moon near perigee adds to the spring tide that the lined-up Sun and Moon raise anyway: the perigean spring tide, or king tide. Over the whole ocean the effect is small, but on some coasts it can be measured, in higher water and in beach erosion.3

The same distance, a saros later

The saros of eclipses (chapter iv) is 223 full moons, 223 × 29.5306 = 6,585.32 days. It is also, nearly enough, 239 anomalistic months: 239 × 27.5546 = … days, … hours longer.4 So after one saros the Moon comes back not only to the same phase and the same node but to almost the same distance, which is why the eclipses of one saros series look so alike.4 The page’s own figures, for the next eclipses and those one saros before them:
