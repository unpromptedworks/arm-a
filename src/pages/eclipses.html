Chapter iv

Eclipses

Every month the Moon passes between the Earth and the Sun, and every month it passes behind the Earth. Only now and then do they line up exactly, and when they do, they keep a calendar of their own.

An eclipse of the Sun needs a new moon, and an eclipse of the Moon needs a full moon. But not every new moon covers the Sun, because the Moon’s orbit is tilted about five degrees to the Earth’s.1 Most months the new moon passes a little above or below the Sun, and the full moon a little above or below the Earth’s shadow. The two orbits cross at two points, the nodes. An eclipse happens only when a new or full moon falls near one of them.

The Sun appears to pass each node once a year, so there are two stretches a year, each lasting about five weeks, when any new or full moon is near enough to a node to be eclipsed. These are the eclipse seasons. Each holds two or three eclipses, and a year can hold as many as seven.1 The nodes slide slowly backwards round the orbit, so the seasons come a little early each year: every 173.31 days, not every half-year.

For eclipses of the Moon it is the fraction of the Moon’s diameter inside the Earth’s dark shadow, the umbra. It is more than 1 when the eclipse is total and less than 0 when the Moon only enters the faint outer shadow, the penumbra. The page cannot say where on Earth an eclipse of the Sun is seen: that needs the shadow’s track, which this chapter does not compute. An eclipse of the Moon is seen from wherever the Moon is up.

The saros

An eclipse depends on three of the Moon’s cycles at once. The new or full moon must fall near a node, which sets the draconic month, from node to node. Its distance, which decides whether an eclipse of the Sun is total or annular, follows the anomalistic month, from perigee to perigee. And the phase follows the synodic month. Chaldean astronomers in Babylon knew a span in which all three come back together, and Edmond Halley gave it its usual name, the saros, in 1686:3

So an eclipse is followed, 6,585⅓ days later (18 years, 11 days and 8 hours, or 10 days if five leap days fall between), by one very like it. The extra third of a day turns the Earth a third of the way round, so the next eclipse of a family is seen about 120 degrees of longitude further west. After three saros, the exeligmos of 54 years and a month, it comes back near the same longitude. The match is not perfect. Each return falls a little further north or south, and a family, a saros series, enters at one edge of the Earth, crosses it, and leaves by the other, between 1,226 and 1,550 years later. Most series hold 71 or 72 eclipses.3 Series are numbered, and the numbers in the table above follow NASA’s.4

How far to trust it

Meeus’s method is short, and it is meant for finding eclipses and their kind, not for planning an expedition. The site’s build checks it against every eclipse of the Sun and Moon NASA lists for 2021 to 2030.4 It finds every one except the eclipse of the Moon on 18 July 2027, which only grazes the penumbra so slightly that no eye could see it. It gets the kind of each one right, including the rare hybrid eclipse of 20 April 2023, which was annular at the ends of its track and total in the middle. It puts the moment of greatest eclipse within about a minute, and the magnitude within 0.01. A search far into the past or future would need a better value of ΔT, the slowly drifting difference between the Earth’s turning and a uniform clock.
