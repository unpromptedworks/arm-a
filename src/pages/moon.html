Chapter i

The Moon

The oldest clock that anyone can read without an instrument, and one that refuses to divide evenly into anything else.

From one new moon to the next takes, on average, 29.53059 days: 29 days, 12 hours, 44 minutes and 3 seconds. This is the synodic month, the month of phases. Single months stray from that mean. The shortest run to about 29.27 days and the longest to about 29.83, because the Moon’s orbit is an ellipse and the Earth’s is too.1

The Moon goes once round the Earth, measured against the stars, in only 27.32 days. The two days’ difference come from the Earth itself. While the Moon makes its circuit, the Earth carries it about a thirteenth of the way round the Sun, and the Moon has to travel a little further to stand in the same line with the Sun again. These are mean phases, from the steady average month. The real Moon runs ahead of or behind the average by up to about half a day, so the true time can fall on the day before or after.2

Nineteen years

Twelve lunar months fall about eleven days short of a year, and thirteen overshoot it, so the phases drift through the calendar. But 235 months come within hours of nineteen years.3 After nineteen years the Moon shows the same face on very nearly the same dates. Meton of Athens introduced the cycle to the Athenian calendar in 432 BC. It still underlies the Hebrew calendar and the calculation of Easter.
