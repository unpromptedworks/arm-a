Chapter i

The Moon

The oldest clock that anyone can read without an instrument, and one that refuses to divide evenly into anything else.

From one new moon to the next takes, on average, 29.53059 days: 29 days, 12 hours, 44 minutes and 3 seconds. This is the synodic month, the month of phases. Single months stray from that mean. The shortest run to about 29.27 days and the longest to about 29.83, because the Moon’s orbit is an ellipse and the Earth’s is too.1

The Moon goes once round the Earth, measured against the stars, in only 27.32 days. The two days’ difference come from the Earth itself. While the Moon makes its circuit, the Earth carries it about a thirteenth of the way round the Sun, and the Moon has to travel a little further to stand in the same line with the Sun again. The true Moon and the mean

The simplest way to find a phase is to count whole average months from one known new moon. Meeus takes the mean new moon of 6 January 2000 and adds 29.530588861 days at a time. That gives the mean phase, the moment an imaginary Moon would reach it if it went round at an even pace. The real Moon does not, and its true phase can fall well over half a day either side.

Two terms do most of the work. The Moon’s orbit is an ellipse, and the Moon runs fastest near perigee, its nearest point. A phase that falls in the fortnight after perigee comes early, and one after apogee comes late, by up to 0.407 of a day at new and full moon, about 9¾ hours. The Earth’s orbit is an ellipse too, so the Sun seems to move fastest across the sky in early January, and the Moon has further to go to catch it. That term is worth up to 0.173 of a day, about 4 hours. At the quarters the Moon’s term is larger, 0.628 of a day, about 15 hours, because the evection, a stretching of the Moon’s orbit by the Sun’s pull, adds to it there. Two dozen smaller terms follow, and fourteen smaller still for the pull of the planets.2

Nineteen years

Twelve lunar months fall about eleven days short of a year, and thirteen overshoot it, so the phases drift through the calendar. But 235 months come within hours of nineteen years.3 After nineteen years the Moon shows the same face on very nearly the same dates. Meton of Athens introduced the cycle to the Athenian calendar in 432 BC. It still underlies the Hebrew calendar and the calculation of Easter.
