Chapter v

The Leap Year

The year of the seasons is not a whole number of days. A calendar that counts in whole days has to keep paying back the fraction, and the rule we use pays it back three times in every four hundred years less often than the old one did.

From one March equinox to the next takes, on average, about 365 days, 5 hours and 49 minutes: the mean tropical year is 365.24219 days.2 A calendar of 365 days falls behind the seasons by almost a quarter of a day each year, and in a century by more than three weeks. The Julian calendar, from 45 BC, mended this with one extra day every fourth year, for an average year of 365.25 days. That is eleven minutes too long, and the error builds to a whole day in about 128 years.1

By the sixteenth century the equinox had crept back to 10 or 11 March, and with it the date of Easter, which is reckoned from 21 March. The reform issued by Pope Gregory XIII in 1582 did two things. It dropped ten days at once, so that Thursday 4 October 1582 was followed by Friday 15 October. And it made three century years in four into common years, so that 400 years hold 97 leap days instead of 100.1

How the equinox wanders

The rule is right on average and wrong in every particular year. Each common year ends about six hours before the Sun has come all the way round, so the equinox arrives about six hours later by the calendar the next year. Each leap day pulls it back by a day. Over a century the four-year saw also slides earlier, because a leap day every fourth year is a little too much. The missing leap day of a century year then pushes everything a day later at once.

The cycle of four hundred years

Four hundred Gregorian years hold 400 × 365 + 97 = 146,097 days, which is exactly 20,871 weeks.1 So the whole calendar, weekdays and all, repeats every four hundred years: 1 January 2401 will be the same weekday as 1 January 2001, and every date in between lines up the same way.

Because the cycle is a whole number of weeks, its dates cannot fall evenly on the seven weekdays. Four hundred years have 4,800 months, and 4,800 is not a multiple of seven, so some weekdays get more thirteenths than others. The page counts them.

Old Style and New

The reform was a law of the Catholic Church, and other states took it up in their own time: Great Britain and its colonies in 1752, by which point the gap had grown to eleven days, and Russia and Greece in the twentieth century, when it was thirteen.1 The gap grows by a day in each century year that the Julian calendar keeps as leap and the Gregorian does not. From 1900 to 2100, a date kept by the Julian calendar, such as Christmas in churches that still follow it, falls thirteen days later by the Gregorian one: 25 December Old Style is 7 January New Style.
