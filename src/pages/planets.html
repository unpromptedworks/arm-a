Chapter ix

The Planets

Five lights wander among the stars and come back: Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn. What returns is not the planet to its place in space but the planet, the Earth and the Sun to the same line, and the time that takes is a cycle of its own.

Each planet goes round the Sun in its own time, its sidereal period. We watch from a moving platform, so what we see repeat is the synodic period: the time between one alignment of Sun, Earth and planet and the next, after the faster of the two has gained one whole lap on the slower. If the planet takes P days to go round and the Earth E, the faster gains 1/P − 1/E of a lap a day, so the synodic period S is given by 1/S = |1/P − 1/E|.2 The table works it out from the mean motions in the Jet Propulsion Laboratory’s elements, with the Earth’s year of 365.25 days.1

Mars is the slow case. It goes round in under two years, but the Earth is chasing it, and it takes more than two years for the Earth to lap it. Jupiter and Saturn go round so slowly that the Earth laps them in little more than a year: Jupiter comes back to the evening sky about a month later each year, and Saturn about a fortnight later.

Where they stand tonight

The angle between a planet and the Sun, as we see it, is its elongation. A planet east of the Sun sets after it and shines in the evening; one west of the Sun rises before it and shines in the morning. Mercury and Venus, inside our orbit, never stray far: Venus reaches between about 45° and 47° from the Sun, and Mercury between about 18° and 28°, as the elongations in the table of the coming year show. They pass between us and the Sun at inferior conjunction and behind it at superior conjunction. The outer planets can stand opposite the Sun, at opposition, when they are nearest, brightest and up all night.

Venus and the eight-year star

Five synodic periods of Venus come to 2,919.6 days, and eight of our years to 2,921.9 days: in eight years Venus returns to the same place in our sky, on nearly the same dates, 2.4 days early. In those eight years Venus itself goes round the Sun thirteen times, in 2,921.1 days.3

Seen from the Earth, Venus draws a loop each time it overtakes us, five loops in the eight years. Seen from the Sun, each inferior conjunction falls on average 144° back round from the last, two-fifths of a circle (the table shows each turn a few degrees either side of it), so joining the five in order of time draws a five-pointed star, the “pentagram of Venus”.4 The two pictures are of the same eight years. After them the star begins again, turned back by 2.5°.

Mars, near and far

Mars comes to opposition every 780 days or so, but its orbit is eccentric, and how near it comes depends on where in that orbit the Earth catches it. Near Mars’s perihelion, at the end of August, the two are nearest; near its aphelion, in February, much farther apart. Oppositions fall about seven weeks later each time round, so a perihelic opposition, the closest kind, comes back after fifteen or seventeen years.5 The chart counts them.

How the positions are found

Each planet’s orbit is given by six elements, its size, shape, tilt, the place of the planet along it at one moment, and the directions of its perihelion and node, each with a slow rate of change. E. M. Standish of the Jet Propulsion Laboratory fitted these to the planetary ephemeris for the years 1800 to 2050.1 For any moment the page sets the elements to that date, solves Kepler’s equation for where the planet is along its ellipse, and turns the ellipse into place. Subtracting the Earth’s position gives the planet as we see it. Conjunctions and oppositions are found by stepping a day at a time until the planet’s longitude crosses the Sun’s or the opposite point, then halving the step forty times. Standish gives the errors over 1800–2050 as about 15 to 40 seconds of arc in longitude for Mercury, Venus, the Earth and Mars, and 400 and 600 for Jupiter and Saturn,1 which moves a conjunction by minutes for the inner planets and by up to a quarter of an hour or so for the outer; the page ignores the travel time of light, which moves it by a few minutes more. The dates here are good to the day, and usually to the hour.
